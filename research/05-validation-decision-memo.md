## 1. MODIFY. The customer stays the scanned company, the price of monitoring is wrong, and the regulation you lead with changes.

Not KILL. The "fatal" verdict in the competition investigation collapsed under its own verifier, and it collapsed on the exact fact that made it fatal: SB 690 does not "delete the CIPA private right of action for website conduct on ~September 30, 2026." September 30 is the governor's signature deadline; with no urgency clause the bill is operative **January 1, 2027**, it reaches **only Penal Code §§638.50/638.51**, and §631/§632.7 private rights of action are untouched. Plaintiffs plead 631 and 638.51 together in the same complaint. Your own `research/04-cipa-reform-and-the-pitch-shift.md` had this right before the investigation got it wrong. The "eight weeks until the fear expires" argument was off by five months and by two of three statutory theories. Discount that entire verdict.

Not GO either, because three things in the plan as written are wrong enough to burn the first quarter.

**Customer: still the scanned company, contracted directly.** Insurers are out — the slot is occupied (LOKKER/Bitsight, March 2025, verified), Coalition and Corvus built it in-house, and entry costs 9–18 months of procurement plus SOC 2, which spends your only scarce resource to arrive second. Law firms are **distribution, not the buyer**. The only clean compensation structure is a nonexclusive, disclosed, *unpaid* mutual referral understanding; a firm sharing legal fees with you is flatly barred by Rule 5.4(a), and you paying the firm creates a Rule 1.7(a)(2) conflict most privacy firms will decline to paper for a $7,500 vendor. Your money comes from the engagement itself.

One structural exception, and it is important: when an account has a **live trigger** — demand letter, CPPA/AG inquiry, M&A diligence — run that engagement through outside counsel, invoiced to the firm and passed through as a disbursement **at actual cost** (ABA Formal Op. 93-379 bars surcharging). Do not rely on the GC to carry privilege. The privilege investigation's central recommendation ("its own GC suffices, one-week rewrite") did not survive its verifier: a GC-commissioned scan of your own site is a textbook dual-purpose engagement, and the Ninth Circuit — where CIPA litigation actually lives — applies the strict primary-purpose test. For untriggered accounts, don't claim privilege at all; sell it openly as ordinary-course compliance work.

**Pricing: keep $7,500, cut monitoring to $1,000–2,000/month.** The $7,500 holds — the comparable is not a $199 CMP tier, it is a law firm's $5k–$25k fixed-fee audit or an expert engagement, and your zero-data-access property really does skip vendor security review. But it only holds if the deliverable contains human analysis and a named, published methodology. A machine-generated HTML file does not survive the fact that Privisy gives away a real-browser GPC scan for free.

The $3,000–6,000/month does not survive contact with a compliance-budget buyer. Privado's Web Auditor is $600/site/month; Osano Enterprise runs roughly $2–3k/month for an entire CMP. Note the flip side the verifiers caught: Osano's $199 is a self-serve floor, not "the full CMP," so the "15–60x" attack was cherry-picked. The honest read is that $3–6k is roughly 2–3x too high, not 30x. Reprice to $1,250–1,500/domain/month with a two-domain floor, and rewrite what monitoring *is*: human-triaged regression review plus a monthly attestation, not a dashboard.

And stop calling it "continuous compliance assurance." A standing weekly scan is the paradigm case of ordinary-course activity — unprivilegeable under any structure, per Capital One and Rutter's — and its archive is a dated record of when the company knew and hadn't fixed it. Two changes: 90-day rolling retention on raw captures, and a remediation-status field on every finding so the archive documents *fixing*, not *knowing*. Never sell one account both a counsel-routed assessment and a monitoring subscription covering the same scope; the subscription contract is the exhibit that destroys privilege over the assessment.

**Regulation: lead with CCPA regulations §7025(c)(6).** More on this in section 4.

## 2. Biggest remaining threat: your outreach is formally indistinguishable from a demand letter, so it converts at zero.

Not commoditization, not SB 690. Delivery in this business is nearly free; distribution *is* the business. And your cold email — "we scanned your site, here is the tracker that fired after you clicked reject" — has the same first sentence as the letters hundreds of brands receive weekly from four volume plaintiff firms using an identical scanning technique. Recipients' counsel have trained them to forward and never reply. A 0% reply rate kills this regardless of how good the scanner is, and you would not be able to tell it apart from bad copy for two months.

Three things de-risk it, in order.

**Publish first, and make it a hard gate, not a phase.** The aggregate sector index ("Pre-Consent Tracking in US Retail, 2026") must exist, be public, and preferably be cited once by counsel or press *before* any named outreach goes out. That converts the email from a threat into research follow-up. You already planned this as weeks 2–4; move it to the gate.

**Route to the operator, not to Legal.** The privilege investigation told you to route outreach to Legal. That is exactly backwards for cold contact — Legal is the function trained to forward it to litigation counsel. Send to whoever owns the tag stack: Director of Marketing Ops, Web Analytics, Privacy Ops. They can verify your finding in five minutes, they are personally embarrassed by it, and they are not conflict-checking you. Privilege only matters once there is a trigger, and by then you are being introduced by counsel anyway.

**Lead the email with the §7025(c)(6) display requirement, not with the tracker.** "Since January 1, 2026 the regulation requires the site to display that an opt-out preference signal was processed; here is what yours displays" is a compliance observation. "Here is the pixel that fired" is a demand letter. Same scan, opposite category.

## 3. Week one: five things, all cheap, all falsifying.

**Run the scanner against 300–500 live domains.** Your own plan says the engine has only been validated against fixtures. Everything downstream — the $457/hour score, the 8-client target, the whole thesis — is conditional on numbers you have not measured. Measure four: percentage that transmit to a third party after a reject click; percentage that ignore GPC; percentage with no reject control at all; and false-positive rate against 20 sites you hand-verify as clean. Decision rules, set now: if post-reject failure is under 20%, the reject-path hook is not a business and the GPC pass leads instead. If false positives exceed 2%, send zero emails until it's fixed.

**Count how many of those sites display the §7025(c)(6) confirmation.** My prediction is near zero. If that holds, it is your lead finding and it is dated, machine-checkable, and universal.

**Diff yourself against the free and enterprise incumbents on five of your own targets.** Run Privisy's free scan, request Lokker's Consent Validator demo, and request pricing from Lokker and Privado as a prospect. You need two answers this week: does a free tool already produce your headline finding, and what is the true enterprise price anchor? Nobody in this dossier could get Lokker's price — it is unpublished, and the verifier specifically could not confirm Lokker decodes `gcs` or Meta LDU at all. That gap is where your remaining technical differentiation lives, and one demo call resolves it.

**Email ten privacy defense firms one question:** if your client gets a §631/§638.51 demand letter, will you retain and pay a scanning vendor and bill it through as a disbursement, or will you tell the client to contract directly? The entire counsel-channel thesis rests on this and no one has tested it.

**Have counsel pull the enrolled SB 690 text.** Nobody in this chain — four researchers, four skeptics, me — has read the actual bill. Every source is law-firm commentary. It costs one email.

## 4. Lead with CCPA regulations §7025(c)(6), sold to the privacy office. Not CIPA, not the EU.

The single best fact anyone surfaced in this whole dossier: effective **January 1, 2026**, §7025(c)(6) changed from "may" to "shall" — a business that processes an opt-out preference signal **must display** that it has done so. Two verifiers confirmed it independently. It is dated, it is new, it is externally observable, it is almost universally unmet, and it maps one-to-one onto your pass 2. Pair it with §7025(c) generally (GPC must be treated as a valid opt-out of sale/sharing).

The supporting evidence is the enforcement ladder, and you must fix it before showing it to a lawyer: **Honda $632,500 (CPPA, March 2025 — not 2026, and not a GPC case; it was symmetry-of-choice, verification friction, and authorized agents), Tractor Supply $1.35M (CPPA, Sept 30 2025, GPC among the violations), PlayOn Sports $1.1M (CPPA, Mar 3 2026), Ford $375,703 (CPPA, Mar 5 2026), Disney $2.75M (California Attorney General Bonta — not the CPPA — Feb 11 2026), GM $12.75M (May 8 2026, AG + DAs + CPPA jointly, largest CCPA penalty to date).** Your current deck attributes Disney to the CPPA, dates Honda to 2026, and omits GM and Tractor Supply — the two largest. Also drop the $2,500/$7,500 penalty figures; they are CPI-indexed and have been $2,663/$7,988 since January 1, 2025. Privacy counsel will catch every one of these, in the exact channel where credibility is the whole asset.

Your dated future catalyst, replacing the expiring one: **AB 566**, signed October 8, 2025, operative January 1, 2027 — browsers must offer an opt-out preference signal. The volume of GPC traffic is about to jump sharply, which makes ignoring it more expensive, not less. That is a real, verified tailwind arriving the same month SB 690 would take a slice out of the litigation side.

Keep CIPA as context in the report's enforcement section, not as the thesis — §631/§632.7 exposure survives SB 690 entirely and demand letters will keep arriving.

**Do not lead with the EU.** Article 50 has no drift property, which your own plan concedes, so it cannot carry a retainer; the AI Act's high-risk timeline slipped to December 2027; and you have no EU distribution. Keep it as a second module you can point at, not a pitch.

## 5. What the plan got wrong.

The plan's proudest line — *"this urgency cannot be legislated away"* — is half wrong in a way that matters. SB 690 would retroactively wipe pending §638.51 claims filed on or after January 1, 2025. That is legislated demand destruction, and it lands on the theory driving the largest share of current filing volume. But the deeper error runs the other way: the plan treats plaintiffs as the engine when the regulator is the engine, and the regulator's obligations are getting *stronger* (may→shall, AB 566) while the litigation side thins.

The moat is not where you think. The plan says the moat is "the maintained tracker corpus." It isn't — Google Consent Mode `gcs` decoding is already free in half a dozen browser extensions (Consent Mode Inspector, ConsentScope, Privacy Bench, UniConsent), Privisy gives away a real-browser GPC scan, and ObservePoint's docs describe GPC-enabled and opted-out audits as standard configurations. Two consolations, both real: **nobody found a Meta Limited Data Use adjudicator**, and no competitor ships your exact three-state configuration — LawsuitGuard runs no-action/reject/accept with **no GPC pass**, Privisy runs GPC with no reject click. You are the only one doing both. That is narrower than "the crawler is the asset," and it is a feature gap, not a moat. Also note LawsuitGuard already hash-seals its output and certifies under FRE 902(13)/(14) — the evidence-packaging position you assumed was open is partially taken, so either match it or stop claiming evidentiary superiority.

Blacklight is not your threat. Source-verified: its collector contains no reference to GPC, `Sec-GPC`, `globalPrivacyControl`, `gcs`, or banner interaction; its three page loads are link-crawl sampling. You defended against the wrong tool. Relatedly, `priv-accept` — cited as proof anyone can rebuild your method in days — only clicks *accept*; grep for reject/deny/decline returns nothing. Reject-click automation is a maintenance treadmill (Consent-O-Matic maintains a standing broken-rules queue for 200+ CMPs). Your build is more defensible than the commoditization argument claimed, and less defensible than "the corpus is the moat" claimed.

Finally, the arithmetic. Eight clients at $480k year one requires nearly every one to take monitoring at $4k+/month. At a defensible $1,500, the same eight clients produce roughly $204k gross, and less in year one because monitoring starts mid-year. That is still an excellent outcome for ~500 founder-hours — but the headline number in the plan is about 2.4x optimistic, and the `score.py` ranking that chose this business over six alternatives used a conversion rate and a failure rate that have never been measured.

**Where I'd bet on the genuinely ambiguous question** — whether regulator-driven demand converts at all, which nobody verified. I'd bet it converts *slower* but *better*: 2–3x longer sales cycle, a buyer with a standing budget line rather than a one-time panic, and materially better retention, because a compliance obligation recurs and a demand letter doesn't. Plan cash for the longer cycle. That is the trade you are making, and it is the right one.
